Cybersecurity Classified Protection: Process, Costs, and Timelines

Eva·2026.08.13·7 min read

 

What the Law Says

MLPS stands for the Multi-Level Protection Scheme for Cybersecurity. Previously called classified protection of information-system security, it was renamed after the Cybersecurity Law took effect in 2017. Article 21 establishes the national cybersecurity classified-protection system and sets out five duties for network operators, including retaining logs for at least six months. Violations that are not corrected are punished under Article 59, starting with orders to correct and warnings, then fines: RMB 10,000–100,000 for organizations and RMB 5,000–50,000 for directly responsible supervisors. These penalties are not enormous, but they draw a line between having acted and having done nothing. After a data breach, regulators look first at that line. Completing the work and passing an assessment counts as fulfilling statutory duties and supports leniency. Failing to do so means knowingly leaving risks unaddressed and invites harsher treatment.

How the Five Levels Work

The system itself is straightforward: classify systems into five levels according to the consequences of an incident, from Level 1, the lowest, to Level 5, the highest. Each level carries different protective requirements, followed by assessment by a qualified third party. The 1.0-era names were discretionary protection, system-audit protection, security-label protection, structured protection, and access-verification protection. The 2.0 standards dropped these older names and simply use Levels 1 through 5.

Most companies fall under Level 2 or Level 3. Level 2 involves guided protection: public-service systems in education, healthcare, and finance, along with most ordinary corporate websites, generally fall here. Level 3 involves supervised protection and covers core financial systems, important government operations, and large platforms, whose compromise harms social order and public interests. Level 4 concerns national security and economic operations. Level 5 covers critical national infrastructure and is rarely implemented publicly, so it has little relevance to ordinary people.

Walking Through the Process

Once the level is determined, follow the normal process. Classification begins with an expert-review meeting and a classification report. Then, within 30 days, file with the cybersecurity department of the local public-security authority at the prefecture level or above and obtain a filing certificate. Next, self-inspect and remediate against the requirements for that level: security equipment, logs, backups, permissions, and emergency plans, one by one. Once remediation is complete, engage a qualified assessment agency to test the system and issue a report. Assessment frequency is explicitly stated: at least annually for Level 3 and at least every six months for Level 4; Level 5 follows relevant national requirements. This rule comes from the Measures for the Administration of Classified Protection of Information Security, jointly issued by the Ministry of Public Security and three other departments in 2007, and remains the prevailing basis. Level 2 has no nationally uniform mandatory frequency; many localities use an interval of roughly two years.

How Assessments Are Scored

Assessments previously used a 100-point scale: above 90 was excellent, above 80 good, above 70 average, and below 70 poor. This changed in 2025. The Cybersecurity Classified Protection Assessment Report Template (2025 Edition) replaced four grades with three conclusions: compliant, substantially compliant, and noncompliant. The judgment depends on indicator compliance rate and major risks. More than 90% compliance with no major risk qualifies as compliant. A rate between 60% and 90%, or a sufficiently high rate accompanied by one major risk—such as insufficient six-month log retention or missing backups—is substantially compliant. Below 60% is noncompliant.

What MLPS 2.0 Changed

MLPS 2.0 is the new standard that took effect on December 1, 2019, designated GB/T 22239-2019, replacing the 2008 edition. Its biggest difference from 1.0 is coverage: cloud computing, mobile internet, the Internet of Things, industrial control systems, and big data each have dedicated extended requirements. Its protection philosophy also changed to one center and three layers of defense: a security-management center links the computing environment, regional boundaries, and communication network, while trusted computing is introduced as well. Cloud operations follow shared responsibility: the cloud platform obtains its own certification, while tenant-built systems undergo separate assessments at their own assigned levels.

Cost and Time

Next comes the question most people care about: money. There is no official price, so these are market estimates. Assessment fees are generally tens of thousands of yuan for Level 2 and roughly RMB 100,000–300,000 for Level 3, depending on region, agency, and system size. Remediation accounts for the bulk of spending: security equipment, log platforms, disaster-recovery backups, and dedicated staff often cost several times the assessment fee. Do not be too optimistic about timing. From classification to a passing report, a smooth process takes two to six months; remediation and rework can stretch it to a year. Level 3 requires annual reassessment, making this a recurring yearly expense rather than a one-time purchase.

Common Criticisms

The most frequent criticism is that MLPS can become a matter of buying a certificate: organizations rush remediation before assessment, then revert afterward, disconnecting compliance from real security. Regulators have been trying to address this through spot checks. Assessment agencies also vary widely in strictness since the market opened up, and some cut corners. Check credentials first; Ministry of Public Security-approved lists can be found on provincial public-security department websites. Do not choose on price alone. Misclassification is another longstanding problem: some raise systems to Level 3 for bids and waste hundreds of thousands of yuan; others classify too low to save money, only to face accountability at a higher level after an incident. Both are traps. Small and micro businesses face the hardest burden: Level 3 investment starting in the hundreds of thousands can overwhelm a small company. Regulators provide flexible arrangements such as filing first and remediating in stages, but local implementation varies.

A Passing Grade

Passing MLPS is far from absolute security. It is the passing grade—the legal minimum. Being breached after passing is not a contradiction: passing a driving test and never breaking a traffic rule are different things.

Practical Advice

Finally, some practical points. Allow enough time: classification to report takes two or three months at best and perhaps half a year. Do not start two months before a bid deadline. Self-inspect and remediate before engaging an assessor; going straight into assessment unprepared will probably fail, costing more time and money. Budget annually: Level 3 reassessment is unavoidable. Keep filing certificates, assessment reports, and remediation records; you need them for bids and as evidence after incidents. The most important step comes after assessment: maintain the logs, backups, permissions, and emergency plans you put in place. If you sustain them, the money buys real security. If not, it buys a sheet of paper.
