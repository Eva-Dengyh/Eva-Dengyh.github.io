An Introduction to Knowledge Graphs: From Relationships to an AI Knowledge Foundation

Eva·2026.05.26·13 min read

 

Introduction

The first time you hear “knowledge graph,” several questions may come to mind. How can knowledge be a graph? How does it differ from a database? What does it have to do with large models, RAG, and intelligent question answering?

Think of it this way: a knowledge graph organizes real-world things and their relationships in a form computers can understand, query, and reason over.

For example, these relationships:

乔布斯 → 创立 → 苹果公司
苹果公司 → 发布 → iPhone
iPhone → 属于 → 智能手机
苹果公司 → 总部位于 → 库比蒂诺

One relationship looks like a fact. Connect many of them and you have a network.

The network's value goes beyond visualization. Machines can use it to follow relationship paths to answers, determine whether objects are related, explain recommendations, uncover chains of potential risk, or supply large models with more solid context.

A knowledge graph is therefore not essentially about drawing an attractive diagram. It is about turning knowledge relationships scattered across tables, documents, webpages, systems, and people's minds into structures computers can understand and reuse.

1. Starting with a search question

Suppose you ask a search engine:

苹果创始人是谁？

Here, “Apple” can mean several things: a fruit, Apple Inc., a project, a brand, or someone's nickname.

The traditional keyword-search approach

The system sees words such as “Apple” and “founder,” matches webpages containing them, and ranks their relevance. The result depends on page popularity and inbound links.

The knowledge-graph approach

The system first determines that “Apple” probably means the entity Apple Inc. It then retrieves directly from the graph:

苹果公司 → 创始人 → 史蒂夫·乔布斯
苹果公司 → 创始人 → 史蒂夫·沃兹尼亚克
苹果公司 → 创始人 → 罗纳德·韦恩

What is the difference?

  • Keyword search guesses which webpage most resembles an answer
  • A knowledge graph uses explicitly structured facts

That is its central advantage over traditional text search:it goes beyond finding words to understanding what a word refers to and how that entity relates to others.

2. What exactly is a knowledge graph?

A one-sentence definition: A knowledge graph is a graph structure that organizes knowledge through entities and relationships.

There are three key elements:

Entities
Identifiable real-world objects: a person, company, book, city, disease, product, or system API can all be entities.

Relationships
Connections between entities, such as:

人 → 任职于 → 公司
公司 → 位于 → 城市
疾病 → 表现为 → 症状
药物 → 治疗 → 疾病
项目 → 依赖 → 系统

Graph structure
Entities become nodes and relationships become edges, forming a queryable network.

Components of a knowledge graph

ElementMeaningExample
EntityIdentifiable objectApple Inc., iPhone, Steve Jobs
RelationshipConnection between objectsFounded, released, belongs to, located in
AttributeAn object's own characteristicFounding year, headquarters address, price, date of birth
TypeThe category an entity belongs toCompany, person, product, city
OntologyRules defining concepts and relationshipsA company can have a founder; a product can belong to a company
SourceWhere a fact comes fromOfficial website, database, document, human review
ConfidenceHow trustworthy a fact is0.95, reviewed, awaiting confirmation

The basic form of knowledge: Triples

Knowledge graphs most commonly express information as triples: subject–predicate–object.

乔布斯 → 创立 → 苹果公司
苹果公司 → 发布 → iPhone
北京 → 是首都 → 中国
阿司匹林 → 可缓解 → 疼痛

Each triple records one fact. Many connected triples form a knowledge graph.

3. Why not just use a database?

People often ask: couldn't this data simply go into a database?

Of course it could. Relational databases excel at storing and managing structured data, including users, orders, products, and employees.

The distinction lies in the design philosophy:a database optimizes organizing data into tables; a knowledge graph optimizes expressing relationships clearly.

A financial risk-control example

A risk-control system might have tables for users, devices, bank cards, phone numbers, transactions, and merchants.

Viewed separately, every user in each table looks normal.

Connect their relationships, however, and hidden risks emerge:

用户A → 使用 → 设备X
用户B → 使用 → 设备X
用户C → 使用 → 设备X

用户A → 绑定 → 银行卡Y
用户B → 收款至 → 银行卡Y

用户A、B、C → 登录自 → 同一IP地址

The risk lies not in a single record, but in the pattern formed by these relationships.

Databases can query relationships with JOINs, but many relationship types, deep traversals, and mixed data sources make schemas complex and difficult to maintain. Knowledge graphs are better suited to questions such as:

  • Who is related to whom?
  • What paths connect them?
  • What does a path mean?
  • Does this set of relationships reveal a risk, opportunity, or dependency?

A knowledge graph does not replace the database; it adds a layer of relationship understanding between databases and applications.

4. Knowledge graphs versus search and vector databases

Search engines, vector databases, and knowledge graphs are all common in modern AI applications. Each has its strengths:

数据库 → 擅长存储精确的记录
搜索引擎 → 擅长找包含关键词的文本
向量数据库 → 擅长找语义相似的内容
知识图谱 → 擅长表达实体之间的关系

An enterprise knowledge-base example

A user asks:上次 Chat 接口为什么重构？

Keyword search finds documents containing “Chat,” “API,” and “refactoring.”

Vector retrieval finds semantically similar passages, such as “API modernization” or “request-path changes.”

A knowledge graph finds documents and also reveals:

  • Which system owns the Chat API
  • Which services it depends on
  • Which errors have occurred historically
  • Which logs, tests, and meeting notes mention it
  • Who has been responsible for that request path
  • Which upstream and downstream systems the refactoring affected

The advantage is organizing context along entity relationships, beyond merely finding similar content.

5. Core knowledge-graph concepts

You need not dive straight into RDF, OWL, or SPARQL. Start by understanding the following concepts.

1. Entities: Identity matters

Entities are a graph's basic nodes. The hard part is not recognizing a word, but identifying the exact object it refers to.

"苹果" → 水果？苹果公司？内部项目代号？
"张伟" → 销售同事？客户联系人？
"Chat" → 页面组件？后端接口？产品模块？

Get identity wrong and every subsequent relationship becomes wrong. The first crucial task is therefore entity recognition and disambiguation.

2. Relationships: Not every line is meaningful

Relationships are the heart of a graph, but two entities cannot be connected arbitrarily. A meaningful relationship must answer:

  • What does it mean?
  • Does it have a direction?
  • Does it have a time range?
  • What is its source?
  • Can the business explain it?

Consider this chain:

员工A → 负责 → 项目B
项目B → 依赖 → 系统C
系统C → 出现 → 错误D
错误D → 记录于 → 日志E

This path clearly explains why Project B is affected. An arbitrary “related to” edge would offer far less business value.

3. Ontology: Defining the rules

Think of an ontology as the knowledge graph's architectural blueprint. It specifies:

  • Which entity types exist?
  • Which relationship types exist?
  • Which relationships are valid? For example, a person may have a birthplace, but a company may not
  • Which attributes are required?
  • What hierarchies exist between entities?

Without ontology constraints, a graph easily becomes a tangle of lines.

4. Sources and confidence: Traceability matters

A production knowledge graph must record the source of every fact.

来自官网 → 高信任
来自数据库 → 中高信任
来自模型自动抽取 → 低信任
来自人工审核 → 最高信任

Especially in finance, healthcare, and law, saying “the graph contains this relationship” is insufficient. You must also explain:

  • Where did it come from?
  • When was it created?
  • Who reviewed it?
  • Is it still valid?
  • How can it be rolled back if wrong?

6. How knowledge graphs are built

Knowledge graphs are not drawn manually one line at a time. The practical process is:

确定业务问题
  ↓
设计 schema / 本体
  ↓
接入数据源
  ↓
抽取实体和关系
  ↓
实体链接与消歧
  ↓
融合多源数据
  ↓
质量校验
  ↓
存储和查询
  ↓
接入应用
  ↓
持续迭代更新

Step 1: Define the business objective

Rather than starting with “I want an enterprise knowledge graph,” ask:

  • What specific questions must it answer?
  • Will it help support agents answer product questions faster?
  • Will it help risk teams detect fraud rings?
  • Will it help engineers understand system dependencies?
  • Will it help doctors find relationships between diseases and medications?

The more concrete the objective, the easier implementation becomes.

Step 2: Design the schema

The schema is the graph's skeleton. For enterprise engineering knowledge, an initial version could simply define:

Entity types: Project, system, API, document, person, error, test record

Relationship types: 

人员 → 负责 → 项目
项目 → 包含 → 系统
系统 → 提供 → 接口
接口 → 出现 → 错误
错误 → 记录于 → 日志
文档 → 描述 → 系统
测试记录 → 验证 → 接口

Key principle: Do not aim for comprehensive coverage initially. Correct connections among core entities matter more than accumulating many types.

Step 3: Extract knowledge from data

Data sources generally fall into three categories:

Structured: Databases, tables, APIs

  • Rules can map them directly into the graph

Semistructured: Webpages, JSON, configuration files

  • Require parsing and transformation

Unstructured: Documents, emails, support tickets, meeting notes

  • Require NLP or large models to extract entities and relationships

Important reminder: Candidate knowledge extracted by a model should not enter the database directly. It still needs rule validation, source tagging, confidence scoring, and human review.

Step 4: Entity disambiguation and fusion

This critical step is often underestimated.

Different systems may use different names for the same object:

IBM = International Business Machines = 国际商业机器公司

The same name can also refer to different objects:

"苹果" → 水果？苹果公司？内部项目？
"张伟" → 销售员工？客户？供应商？

Poor handling leads to:

  • Incorrect merges that bind unrelated facts together
  • Incorrect splits that fragment knowledge about one object

Entity disambiguation and fusion are therefore central to graph quality.

Step 5: Integrate applications and iterate

Building the graph is only the beginning. Connect it to real applications: search, question answering, recommendations, risk control, and data governance.

Then continuously improve it using feedback:

  • What did users click?
  • Was the answer accepted?
  • Which relationships are frequently missing?
  • Which facts have expired?
  • Which entities were created twice?

This feedback drives iterative improvement.

7. Knowledge-graph applications

Knowledge graphs suit scenarios with complex relationships, a need for explanations, and multiple data sources.

Search

When Google introduced Knowledge Graph in 2012, it used the memorable phrase:

Things, not strings—understanding things, rather than just strings.

When you search for a person, place, film, or company, the engine can return structured profiles, related entities, and historical events alongside webpages. Knowledge graphs make this possible.

Recommendation systems

E-commerce recommendations go beyond “people who bought A also bought B.” A knowledge graph can understand:

  • Which category a product belongs to
  • Relationships between brands
  • Users' preferences in different situations
  • Compatibility between accessories and devices

These relationships make recommendations easier to explain and trust.

Financial risk control

A risk graph connects users, accounts, devices, bank cards, phone numbers, IP addresses, merchants, and transactions.

It excels at discovering fraud rings hidden across multiple hops: accounts sharing devices or receiving cards, logging in from the same IP, or funneling funds to one merchant. Graph structures make these patterns easier to identify.

Healthcare and pharmaceuticals

Medical knowledge naturally suits graphs: diseases, symptoms, drugs, genes, examinations, guidelines, and contraindications have explicit relationships.

However, medical graphs impose extremely strict sourcing and review requirements. Automatically generated model output cannot be treated as medical fact.

Enterprise knowledge management

This is a particularly promising new direction.

Enterprise knowledge is scattered across documents, code, tickets, chats, meeting notes, and configuration. Knowledge graphs can connect projects, systems, APIs, people, documents, decisions, and incidents.

When an employee asks a question, the system goes beyond finding similar documents to organizing an answer through the enterprise knowledge network.

Large models and GraphRAG

Large models have opened new applications for knowledge graphs.

Traditional RAG flow: 

文档切块 → 向量检索 → 召回文本 → 大模型生成答案

This works well for single-document questions, but may miss essential context when questions span documents, entities, and multihop relationships.

The GraphRAG approach: 

识别实体和关系
  → 构建图结构
  → 沿着图谱找相关上下文
  → 交给大模型生成答案

Knowledge graphs do not replace large models; they give them a sturdier factual framework.

8. Conclusion

A knowledge graph can be understood simply as:

A machine-readable network of facts and relationships

In depth, however, it involves much more than connecting entities: identity, relationship semantics, ontology constraints, provenance, confidence, permissions, versioning, and application feedback loops all matter.

The real value of a knowledge graph is therefore not drawing data as a network, but making scattered, implicit, hard-to-reuse relationships explicit.

In search, it moves systems from matching strings to understanding entities.

In enterprises, it moves the business from isolated data to relationship insights.

In the large-model era, it helps AI move from generating text to reasoning with structured context.

Deciding whether a scenario needs a knowledge graph

When the essential issue lies in relationships between objects rather than individual records, and answers must explain their sources and paths as well as provide results, knowledge graphs become valuable.
