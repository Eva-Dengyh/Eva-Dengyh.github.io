Industry Analysis, Decision Challenges, and Code Acceptance: Three Skills for a Cognitive Exoskeleton

Eva·2026.04.21·14 min read

 

Abstract: Instead of collecting "all-purpose prompts," package frequent thinking processes into a Skill—a reusable expert workflow. This article records three Skills I wrote, their shared methodology, and how anyone can get started.

Skills and Repository Covered in This Article

The complete definitions and source files for these three Skills are open source in my notes repository, for reference and adaptation:

SkillPurpose
Industry X-RayExpert in deep industry analysis
Devil's AdvocateA shadow opponent before major decisions
Skill AcceptorAn acceptance-briefing protocol for AI-generated code

Skill files are in the repository's skills/ directory (such as industry-xray-teardown, devils-advocate, and skill-acceptor), matching the names used in this article.

Recently I did something small but, to me, interesting: instead of writing another collection of "all-purpose prompts," I wrote the three AI Skills above.

They seem to serve entirely different purposes: researching industries, challenging decisions, and accepting code. But after writing them, I realized they all point to the same question:

How do we turn AI from a chat tool into an expert counterpart that is truly reusable, capable of delivering work, and collaborative?

This article explores how I wrote these three Skills and the methodology they share.

I. Why Prompts Alone No Longer Satisfy Me

Many people still use AI through one-line requests:

"Analyze this industry for me."
"Tell me whether this decision makes sense."
"Summarize this code for me."

These questions produce answers, of course, but the problem is obvious:inconsistent output. Sometimes AI is insightful, sometimes perfunctory; sometimes well structured, sometimes full of correct but empty statements. Sometimes it helps you judge; sometimes it merely agrees with you.

The reason is simple: you gave it a task, but no working protocol.

The value of a Skill is precisely that it turns a one-off exchange into a reusable expert workflow. A good Skill does more than tell AI who it is. It specifies:

  • When to activate
  • What qualifies as acceptable input
  • Which reasoning steps to follow
  • Which behaviors are prohibited
  • The required final output format
  • How to continue interacting with the user

In other words, a Skill is an operating manual, not a magic incantation.

II. Skill One: An Industry X-ray That Translates Jargon into Plain Language

The first Skill is called Industry X-Ray, positioned as an expert in deep industry analysis.

It solves a common problem: unfamiliar industries can overwhelm us with jargon—SaaS, MCNs, new energy, cross-border commerce, short-form dramas, chips, AI agents. Industry reports are thick, expert interviews opaque, and media coverage piles on concepts. After reading everything, you may remember many terms but still not know how the industry makes money, who earns the big profits, who does the hard work, or who will be disrupted next.

So I designed a very explicit analytical framework for this Skill.

1. Start in Plain Language: What Does This Industry Actually Do?

It must explain the industry's essence in one accessible, perhaps slightly humorous sentence. For example:

SaaS is essentially the software world's "bike sharing": instead of paying a large upfront price to own software, you pay monthly rent and use it whenever you need.

This requirement matters: if the industry's basic reason for existing cannot be explained plainly, everything that follows may be nothing more than jargon.

2. Peel Back Three Layers: The Surface, the Mechanics, and the Foundations

I divide the industry into three layers:

LayerFocus
SurfaceCurrent landscape, leading players, common jargon
MiddleValue chain, money flows, and who earns money the hard way
FoundationFirst principles, core drivers, and variables that could reshuffle the industry

This structure prevents AI from stopping at empty claims like "the industry has great potential" or "competition is fierce." It must answer harder questions: who is upstream and midstream, who pays downstream, where the money comes from, who captures the profits, whether the industry's real foundation is policy, capital, technology, traffic, or human nature, and which future variable could overturn the entire game.

3. Finish by Challenging the User

Another design choice I particularly like:The Skill cannot stop after producing its report. It must ask two scenario-based questions for the user to answer in their own words, such as:

  • Given RMB 5 million, which part of the value chain would you invest in, and why?
  • If a new technology becomes widespread, which type of company fails first?

This may seem unnecessary, but it is crucial: real understanding is not "I followed the explanation"; it is "I can make a judgment in a new situation."AI should be a thinking coach as well as a content generator.

III. Skill Two: A Shadow Opponent That Dismantles Self-Persuasion Before Major Decisions

The second Skill is called Devil's Advocate, which I translate as "shadow opponent." Its temperament is deliberately unappealing:No encouragement, consolation, or emotional reassurance. It does just one thing:

Before a major decision, it coldly plays the clearest-headed opponent in the room.

Perhaps you plan to quit and start a business, spend your savings opening a shop, move cities, make a concentrated investment, or join an early-stage venture. The greatest danger is often not a lack of information, but that we feel the impulse first and then seek reasons to prove ourselves right: classic confirmation bias.

This Skill therefore focuses on just two things: the worst-case scenario through a premortem, and the traps along the execution path through stage-by-stage risks. It closes with two pressure-test questions.

1. Premortem: Put the Worst Case on the Table First

It does not gently ask, "What risks does this plan have?" It starts from an assumption:

It is now one to three years later. Your plan has completely failed, the money is gone, the opportunity cost is enormous, and your reputation may even be damaged.

It then works backward to identify five critical points that caused the failure—a premortem. It forces us to switch from "I will succeed" to "Why will I fail?" and must cover several fatal vulnerabilities:

  • Where cash flow breaks
  • Where capabilities do not match requirements
  • Where the external environment changes abruptly
  • Risks in the team or partnership
  • How the final blow happens

This instantly brings many romantic narratives back to reality.

2. Execution Risks: Defuse Traps Stage by Stage

A catastrophic ending alone is not enough. Many ventures die in the stretch between spending the first money and reaching supposed stability. The Skill therefore follows your plan's actual execution sequence—preparation, launch, ramp-up, or whatever stages fit—and lists each stage's most likely failure points. Wherever possible, it writes "what may happen → what it could turn into," rather than a vague "watch out for risk."

What you receive is not an essay of empty truths, but a risk map aligned with the timeline.

3. End with Hard Questions

It ends not with "Good luck," but with two stress-test questions, such as:

  • If revenue is zero for the first three months, how many months can your cash flow last?
  • If a giant enters tomorrow with heavy subsidies, what concrete defenses do you have besides passion?

If you cannot answer, do not rush to donate your money to reality. I wrote this Skill to make AI willing, at crucial moments, to disagree with me, rather than merely become better at encouragement.

IV. Skill Three: Code Acceptance Without Drowning Humans in AI-generated Code

The third Skill is called Skill Acceptor, and addresses a more engineering-focused situation:How should humans evaluate a large body of code after an AI agent has written it?

This question will grow increasingly important. Code will not become scarcer; it will be generated faster. Previously, the problem was being unable to write it. Now it may be too much code for humans to review. An agent can change more than a dozen files in minutes. Humans who continue reviewing line by line will quickly face cognitive overload.

So Skill Acceptor has a clear goal:

Instead of making humans read undigested low-level boilerplate, have AI produce an architecture-level acceptance briefing.

1. First, Understand the Scope of Changes

It must first answer: where are the main entry points? Which core files were added or modified? Did database schemas change? Were new dependencies introduced? Was global configuration modified? Establish the boundaries first so the risks become visible.

2. Then Filter Out Low-value Code

The rules:Do not explain basic syntax; do not paste large blocks of raw code; do not list files in a mechanical running commentary. What humans need is:

  • What does this code mean for the business?
  • How does the main workflow run, and how does data move?
  • Where are transactions, locks, retries, and fallbacks used?
  • What is returned when something fails?

3. Produce an Acceptance Dashboard

The output resembles a console dashboard: functionality, entry points, a change overview, the core implementation approach, main execution flow, exception and boundary defenses, and code anchors for every key step. For example:

Step 1: An interceptor validates the token and extracts UserId
Step 2: Query inventory and user information in parallel
Step 3: Start a transaction, write the order, and decrement inventory
Step 4: Return 200 while asynchronously sending an MQ message

Every step must include file and function names, so humans can locate crucial code in one click without getting lost in a sea of implementation.

The judgment behind this Skill is:

In the AI era, code review will shift from reading every line toward reviewing architectural intent, business workflows, and boundary defenses.

Humans should focus on higher-level judgment instead of competing with machines on patience.

V. What These Three Skills Share: Turning AI into an Expert with a Process

The scenarios differ, but the underlying structures are similar. My conclusion:A genuinely useful Skill needs at least six elements.

#ElementExplanation
1Define a role, but go beyond itThe role is the skin; the process is the skeleton. Specify how to break things down, what to examine and in what order, what must be asked, and what must not be done.
2Set an entry gate and reject vague inputFor example, Devil's Advocate requires a concrete plan: "I want to start a business" is insufficient; "quit my job and use RMB 500,000 to open an independent cafe in a third-tier city" qualifies.
3Require structured reasoningIndustry X-Ray analyzes industries in three layers; Devil's Advocate uses a premortem plus stage-by-stage execution risks; Skill Acceptor covers change scope, main workflows, and boundary defenses.
4Specify prohibitions to prevent shortcutsFor example: no empty jargon, no mere emotional reassurance, no large code dumps, and no overlooking transactions or exceptions.Constraints often matter more than instructions.
5Fix the output format so results are reusableFormatting is not decoration. It lowers collaboration costs by making output archivable, comparable, and transferable—a work asset.
6Add an interactive feedback loopFinal challenges or acceptance checks force the user to perform an act of thinking. One-way output easily creates an illusion of understanding.

VI. Writing Skills Means Writing Your Own Operating System for Thinking

After writing these three Skills, my strongest impression was:A Skill is more than prompt engineering; it packages a person's way of thinking into a callable module.

  • When writing Industry X-Ray, I was defining how I want to understand an unfamiliar industry.
  • When writing Devil's Advocate, I was defining how I want to counter impulse, optimism, and survivorship bias.
  • When writing Skill Acceptor, I was defining how I want to accept code output in the AI era.

Writing a Skill does more than teach AI how to work. It forces you to clarify what you truly value, your judgment criteria, how you define a good result, which low-quality outputs you dislike, and which thinking practices you want reproduced reliably.

Seen this way, a Skill is a cognitive exoskeleton for a person, not merely a plugin for AI.

VII. How Can Anyone Start Writing Their Own Skills?

If you want to begin, avoid aiming for something all-encompassing immediately. Start with three questions.

1. Which Judgments Do You Make Repeatedly?

Examples include choosing topics, screening resumes, analyzing investments, building customer profiles, reviewing product requirements, organizing meeting notes, reviewing code, and analyzing business models.Anything you do repeatedly that still consumes substantial mental effort is a good candidate for a Skill.

2. What Are Your Standards for a Good Result?

Do not just write "analyze this more deeply." Specify required sections, questions that must be answered, situations that require follow-up, risks that must be listed separately, and outputs that are unacceptable.The more specific you are, the more consistent AI becomes.

3. Whom Should AI Work Like?

Go beyond "an expert" to something more concrete:

  • A sharp-tongued but dependable opponent
  • A teacher who translates jargon into plain language
  • A technical lead focused on architectural intent
  • An exceptionally demanding product reviewer
  • An interviewer skilled at probing motivations

The clearer the role, the clearer the boundaries.

Closing Thoughts

We used to say that people who ask better questions use AI better. The next divide may go beyond question-asking:

Have you turned your frequent thinking processes into reusable Skills?

A prompt addresses one conversation; a Skill addresses a class of problems. Someone who continually packages judgment, experience, and methodology into Skills is doing more than using AI: they are training a set of expert counterparts that understand them increasingly well.

  • When researching an industry, someone strips away the jargon.
  • Before a major decision, someone coldly argues against you.
  • When accepting code, someone translates complex implementation into an architectural briefing.

This does not replace thinking; it helps you uphold a higher standard of thinking. That is what I gained from writing these three Skills. On the surface they are tools; in practice, they are three cognitive extensions I gave myself: one helps me understand a complex world, one counters blind optimism, and one manages code complexity in the AI era.

If AI is an increasingly powerful engine, Skills are the steering wheel, brakes, and dashboard.

  • Without Skills, you are simply chatting with AI.
  • With Skills, you begin building your own working system.
